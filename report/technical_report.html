DocInsights 2026 Shared Task · Dr.DocBench track · System description

pranshu29 at Dr.DocBench 2026: Consensus Selection over Prompt-Diverse VLM Transcriptions for Expert-Level Document Parsing

Pranshu Rastogi and Rajneesh Tiwari, Independent Researchers · Team pranshu29 · Final rank 7 · Overall 84.54

Abstract

We describe a training-free system for the Dr.DocBench document-parsing challenge. A general vision-language model transcribes each page several times under different prompts, image settings and window sizes. A page-level minimum-Bayes-risk (MBR) selector, whose utility is the challenge's own text and reading-order matching, picks one transcription per page. A small set of rules derived from the evaluator's matching behaviour then refines it, and a word-level consensus layer corrects recognition slips that independent OCR engines confirm. Display formulas and tables are evaluated by best match, so we also emit LaTeX and HTML candidate blocks for each page. Our final submission scores 84.54 overall (Text_ED 0.1641, TEDS 71.86, CDM 81.84, Reading Order 85.65, rank 7). We report which choices transferred from the development set to the test set, where the remaining error sits, and a set of measured negative results, including five stronger commercial models that did not improve on the ensemble.

1. Introduction

This report describes our system for the Dr.DocBench track of the DocInsights 2026 shared task (DocInsights Workshop Organizers, 2026), held with the Workshop on Document Intelligence and Understanding at EMNLP 2026. The shared task has two tracks: DocSem, document-grounded quantitative reasoning with evidence attribution (Oracle, 2026), and Dr.DocBench, expert-level parsing of complex document pages (Yang et al., 2026; 2077AI and Abaka AI, 2026). Teams were ranked on the Dr.DocBench leaderboard hosted on EvalAI (EvalAI, 2026).

Dr.DocBench (Yang et al., 2026) selects book pages on which current parsers disagree, and its references follow annotator conventions that vary from book to book: which lines form one block, whether text inside a picture is transcribed, whether a list is a table, and where running headers and page numbers sit in the reading order. Our approach treats the problem as selection under uncertainty. Instead of training a parser, we generate many plausible transcriptions with one capable model and choose among them with minimum-Bayes-risk selection (Kumar and Byrne, 2004), using the metric itself as the utility. All tuning used the development set (986 pages, 66 books) scored with a local copy of the official md2md scorer; the 509 test pages were used only through leaderboard submissions.

2. Related work

Benchmarks. Document-parsing benchmarks moved from layout detection, as in DocLayNet (Pfitzmann et al., 2022) and M6Doc (Cheng et al., 2023), to end-to-end page parsing with block-level annotations, as in OmniDocBench (Ouyang et al., 2025), whose md2md scorer this challenge uses, and to OCR-centred evaluation of multimodal models in OCRBench v2 (Fu et al., 2025). Dr.DocBench (Yang et al., 2026) samples pages on which strong parsers disagree and adds expert content such as chemistry, music and dense formulas. Its metrics combine normalised edit distance (Levenshtein, 1966), TEDS for tables (Zhong et al., 2020) and CDM for formulas (Wang et al., 2025).

Parsing systems. Pipeline toolkits combine layout detection with specialised recognisers: MinerU (Wang et al., 2024), PaddleOCR-VL (Cui et al., 2025, 2026) and Docling (Auer et al., 2024). Compact end-to-end parsers include MinerU2.5 (Niu et al., 2025), MinerU2.5-Pro (Wang et al., 2026), DeepSeek-OCR 2 (Wei et al., 2026) and GLM-OCR (Duan et al., 2026). General vision-language models such as Kimi K2.5 (Kimi Team et al., 2026) also transcribe pages when prompted, and the Dr.DocBench study found the strongest general models ahead of the specialised parsers on text and reading order (Yang et al., 2026).

Combining outputs. Selecting among system outputs with minimum Bayes risk (Kumar and Byrne, 2004) and voting at the word level after alignment, as in ROVER (Fiscus, 1997), are established in machine translation and speech recognition. We apply both to page transcriptions, using the challenge's matching as the utility and Hungarian assignment (Virtanen et al., 2020) as implemented in the scorer.

3. Task and evaluation

The development set has 986 pages from 66 books with Markdown and layout references; the test set has 509 pages from 34 books, five of whose subjects (Drama, Health & Fitness, Juvenile Fiction, Nature, Travel) never occur in development. Each page is converted to Markdown and scored by the md2md pipeline of the official scorer, derived from OmniDocBench (Ouyang et al., 2025). The scorer splits our page and the reference into blocks, matches text blocks by normalised edit distance (Levenshtein, 1966), and reports four components. Text_ED and reading order are computed on matched text blocks; display formulas are scored by CDM (Wang et al., 2025) and tables by TEDS (Zhong et al., 2020), each against its best-matching prediction. The overall score is the mean of the components available on a page, averaged over pages. Reading the scorer closely gave three facts that shaped the system:

  • Consecutive predicted blocks are joined to one reference block only when that block is at most 300 normalised characters. Above that length a segmentation mismatch cannot be repaired by the matcher.
  • Our unmatched text blocks cost nothing while any reference block is unmatched, but once every reference block is matched they are joined into one record and counted in full.
  • Normalisation keeps only word characters (runs of four or more underscores become four), so punctuation and Markdown emphasis do not count, while capitalisation and digits do.

4. System

4.1 Transcription runs

The base model is a general vision-language model served through OpenRouter (OpenRouter, 2026b; Section 7). Pages are sent as two-page windows at 1600 px on the long side, temperature 0. The prompts evolved through several versions; the ones in the final pool add these conventions in turn:

  • v6: copy capitalisation exactly; never write placeholders for illegible text.
  • v7: write page furniture (running headers, footers, page numbers) after the body, in a labelled section, because the references place it last.
  • v10a / v10b / v11: read multi-column pages column by column and start a new paragraph at a column break; v10b also splits paragraphs interrupted by pictures; v11 draws PaddleOCR-VL layout frames on the image.

Further runs vary contrast preprocessing, image size (1200 px) and window size (one page). PaddleOCR-VL (Cui et al., 2025) and the MinerU pipeline (Wang et al., 2024) provide layout blocks, tables and formulas. The final candidate pool for selection has eight transcriptions per page: five base runs (three prompts, a contrast variant, PaddleOCR-VL) and the three column-break runs.

4.2 Page-level rules

Every candidate passes through the same deterministic rules, each kept only if a leave-one-out ablation on the development set showed it helping:

4.3 MBR selection

Following minimum-Bayes-risk decoding (Kumar and Byrne, 2004), for each page we choose the candidate with the highest average agreement with the other runs, where agreement between two candidates is the official text and reading-order matching computed with one of them standing in as the reference. This replaces a character-level medoid and adds about one point on the development set. Adding the three column-break runs as candidates and the six column-break variants as extra pseudo-references raised development Text_ED from 0.1264 to 0.1168 and reading order from 87.79 to 88.58. On the leaderboard this change gave +0.60 overall (Text_ED −0.0048, reading order +0.74).

4.4 Word-level consensus

In the spirit of ROVER (Fiscus, 1997), the other runs' versions of each paragraph of the selected page are aligned word by word. A word changes only when a strict majority agrees, the new spelling is close to the old (an OCR slip, not a different word), the change is not capitalisation alone, and an engine outside our model family (PaddleOCR-VL or MinerU) has the new word and not the old one, or the old word is not a dictionary word. A plain majority vote lost 0.06 on development because our runs share one model and therefore share errors; the gated version is small but never hurt a page.

4.5 Formula and table candidate blocks

Display formulas and tables are evaluated by best match: each reference formula or table is paired with its closest predicted one, and predicted blocks that match nothing are ignored. Display formulas also take no part in text or reading-order matching. The references write many styled lines (italic captions, bold labels, stage directions) as display formulas such as $\textit{…}$. We therefore append to each page standalone LaTeX candidates: every line of the page in the wrapper styles the references use (\textit, \textbf, bold italic, \text, underlined), the same lines from the other candidate runs, and inline formulas of all runs written as display formulas. Tables are handled the same way, with alternative transcriptions (PaddleOCR-VL, crop-level transcriptions, other runs) included as additional HTML tables. These blocks do not change the page's text or its reading order; their effect on the formula score is traced in Table 2.

6. Analysis

6.1 What transferred from development to test

Changes that select among existing transcriptions transferred well: the column-break MBR kept 68% of its development gain overall, 94% for reading order and about 50% for text. Changes that touched most pages or encoded a development-book convention reversed on test: adding contrast-enhanced runs as extra votes (−0.84), replacing two-page with one-page transcriptions (−0.63), and paragraph splits inside list blocks, which helped development books and cost text on test.

6.2 Where the error is

We located test error by blanking groups of books and decoding the leaderboard change. Books whose subjects never appear in development and books on which our runs disagree most (240 pages) held 56% of the text error and most of the reading-order error, at Text_ED 0.20 and reading order 77–81, against 0.12–0.16 and 85–88 for the rest. On development, 40% of the remaining text error is text we write that the reference does not keep, and half of all text error sits on 8% of pages where the reference made a book-specific choice: picture text kept as an image, a directory kept as a table, crossword clues kept as one block, a page whose reference is only its page number. Our words on those pages are right; the choice is not visible in the image or in our runs.

8. Conclusion

A single capable model, prompted several ways and filtered by an MBR selector that uses the evaluation metric as its utility, outperformed every stronger model we tested on this benchmark. Selection-based changes transferred to the hidden test set; rules built on development-book conventions did not. The remaining gap to the leading teams is in segmentation and reading-order conventions that vary by book, which a future system could address with layout-aware models trained on the annotation conventions themselves.
